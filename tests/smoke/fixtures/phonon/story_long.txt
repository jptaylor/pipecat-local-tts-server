The journey began on a bright Thursday morning. I caught the eight fifteen train and found a quiet seat near the window. A woman across the aisle was reading a thick novel, while her young son played with a small toy car.

We passed through several villages before reaching the coast. The view was stunning: white cliffs rose sharply from the azure water, and fishing boats rocked gently in the harbour. I took a few photographs to share with friends back home.

At the hotel, the manager greeted me with a warm smile. She showed me to a comfortable room with a view of the square below. The bathroom had fresh towels and a large mirror above the sink.

That evening, I enjoyed a leisurely meal at a restaurant near the theatre. The fish was excellent, and the service was thoughtful without being rushed. Afterward, I walked along the pier as the sun set behind the distant hills.

On Friday I woke early and followed a narrow path up to the cliffs. The grass was wet with dew, and a cool breeze carried the smell of salt and seaweed. Halfway up, I stopped to watch a pair of gulls circling above the rocks. From the top, the whole bay opened out below me, calm and silver in the morning light.

Back in the village, I met an old fisherman mending his nets on the quay. He told me that his family had worked these waters for four generations. When I asked about the weather, he squinted at the sky and promised a perfect afternoon. He offered to take me out on his boat, and I accepted without hesitation.

We headed out past the lighthouse, and the water turned from green to deep blue. He pointed out a colony of seals resting on a distant sandbank. I leaned over the side to watch them slip into the waves, one after another. By the time we returned, my face was tingling from the sun and the spray.

After lunch, I walked to the far end of the beach and sat on a smooth, warm rock. The tide was going out, and small pools appeared among the stones. I watched a crab scuttle sideways from one shadow to another. For a long while I simply listened to the waves and let my mind go quiet.

On Friday night, the whole village gathered on the quay for a small festival. Someone played an accordion, and children ran between the tables carrying paper lanterns. I shared a bench with a retired teacher who told me stories about the history of the town. We talked until the lanterns burned low and the stars came out above the water.

On Saturday it rained all day, so I wandered through the little museum beside the church. There were old maps, rusted anchors, and faded photographs of sailors in woollen jackets. In the afternoon I found a cafe with steamy windows and ordered hot chocolate and a slice of lemon cake. I wrote three postcards while the rain drummed softly on the roof.

Later, the rain finally stopped, and a pale rainbow stretched across the harbour. I stepped outside and joined a few neighbours who had gathered to watch it. A small dog splashed happily through the puddles, and everyone laughed. It felt like the whole town was breathing out after a long, grey day.

That night, the retired teacher invited me to supper at her house near the church. Her husband had made a thick fish stew, and the kitchen smelled of garlic and fresh bread. They showed me old photographs of the harbour, and we laughed at their stories of storms and shipwrecks. When I finally left, she pressed a small paper bag of biscuits into my hands.

Early on Sunday, someone slid a note under my door. It was from the manager, who wrote that breakfast would be served on the terrace. I carried my coffee outside and sat in the sunshine, watching the square slowly wake up. A baker was setting out trays of warm rolls, and the church bells rang eight times.

After breakfast, I spent the last hours buying small gifts at the market. I chose a blue ceramic mug for my sister and a jar of honey for my neighbour. The stallholder wrapped everything in brown paper and tied it with string.

Then I packed my bag, thanked the manager, and said goodbye to the square. Before leaving the village, I climbed the hill behind the hotel one last time. The air was clear, and I could see the lighthouse, the quay, and the tiny boats in the harbour. I took a final photograph and tried to memorise the view.

On the way to the station I passed the fisherman once more. He raised a hand and shouted that I should return in the spring, when the first boats set out. I promised that I would, and I meant it.

Would you believe I almost missed my train home? I had to rush through the station, but I made it just in time. What a relief that was!

I found a seat by the window and let my breath slow down. A teenager across from me was listening to music, and an elderly couple shared a flask of tea. Outside, the fields rolled by in soft shades of green and gold. I took out my postcards and smiled, because I had forgotten to post them.

As the coast faded behind us, I watched the cliffs shrink into a thin white line. I felt tired, sunburnt, and completely happy. The trip had been simple, but every small moment had stayed with me. I already knew that I would come back to this quiet harbour one day.
